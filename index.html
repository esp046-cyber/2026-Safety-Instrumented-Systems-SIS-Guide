What is a Safety Instrumented System (SIS)?

A Safety Instrumented System (SIS) is a critical control system designed to prevent or mitigate hazardous events in industrial processes. It operates independently from the Basic Process Control System (BPCS) to ensure safety when process conditions deviate from safe operating limits.

2026 Update:

Latest standards emphasize digitalization, cybersecurity integration (IEC 62443), and AI-assisted diagnostics while maintaining fail-safe principles.

Key Components of SIS

  • Sensors: Detect hazardous conditions (pressure, temperature, level, flow, gas detection)
  • Logic Solver: PLC or DCS-based system that processes sensor inputs and executes safety logic
  • Final Elements: Valves, motors, or other devices that take action to move process to safe state
  • Communication Networks: Secure data transfer between components
Critical Principle:

SIS must be INDEPENDENT from BPCS to ensure that common cause failures don't compromise safety. This includes separate sensors, logic, and final elements.

Safety Instrumented Function (SIF)

A Safety Instrumented Function is a safety function with a specified Safety Integrity Level (SIL) that is implemented by a SIS to achieve or maintain a safe state for the process.

Key Requirement:

Each SIF must have a documented SIL requirement based on risk analysis and must be capable of achieving that SIL through design, installation, and testing.

SIF Components & Architecture

Every SIF consists of three subsystems:

SIF = Sensor Subsystem + Logic Solver + Final Element Subsystem PFDavg(SIF) = PFDavg(Sensors) + PFDavg(Logic) + PFDavg(Final Elements)
  • Sensor Subsystem: Detects abnormal process condition
  • Logic Solver: Processes signal and determines if action required
  • Final Element: Takes action to achieve safe state
SIF Development Process ▼

Step 1: Identify Hazard
Conduct HAZOP, LOPA, or other hazard analysis to identify scenarios requiring protection.

Step 2: Determine SIL Target
Calculate required risk reduction and assign SIL (1, 2, 3, or 4).

Step 3: Define SIF Specification
Document: initiating events, safe state, demand mode (low/high), demand rate, response time.

Step 4: Design SIF Architecture
Select redundancy (1oo1, 1oo2, 2oo3, etc.) and components to meet SIL.

Step 5: Calculate PFDavg
Verify design meets SIL requirement through reliability calculations.

Step 6: Proof Test Planning
Establish test intervals and procedures to maintain SIL performance.

Safety Integrity Level (SIL) Overview

SIL is a discrete level (1, 2, 3, or 4) for specifying the safety integrity requirement of a SIF. Higher SIL = lower failure probability = greater risk reduction.

SIL Requirements Table

SIL PFDavg (Low Demand) RRF PFH (High Demand)
SIL 4 10⁻⁵ to < 10⁻⁴ 100,000 to < 10,000 10⁻⁹ to < 10⁻⁸
SIL 3 10⁻⁴ to < 10⁻³ 10,000 to < 1,000 10⁻⁸ to < 10⁻⁷
SIL 2 10⁻³ to < 10⁻² 1,000 to < 100 10⁻⁷ to < 10⁻⁶
SIL 1 10⁻² to < 10⁻¹ 100 to < 10 10⁻⁶ to < 10⁻⁵

Legend:

  • PFDavg: Probability of Failure on Demand (average)
  • RRF: Risk Reduction Factor = 1/PFDavg
  • PFH: Probability of dangerous Failure per Hour
Critical Insight:

PFDavg represents the average unavailability of the SIF. A SIL 2 system (PFDavg = 0.001 to 0.01) will fail to operate on demand 0.1% to 1% of the time.

SIL Determination Methods ▼

1. Risk Graph (IEC 61511)
Semi-quantitative method considering consequence, occupancy, probability of avoiding hazard, and demand rate.

2. Risk Matrix
Maps severity vs. likelihood to determine tolerable risk and required SIL.

3. Layer of Protection Analysis (LOPA)
Most common quantitative method. Calculates initiating event frequency, consequence severity, and IPLs (Independent Protection Layers) to determine required RRF.

4. Calibrated Risk Graph
Company-specific risk graph calibrated to risk tolerance criteria.

Formula: Required RRF = (Initiating Event Frequency × Consequence) / Tolerable Risk

PFDavg Calculation Formulas

1oo1 Architecture (Single Channel):

PFDavg = (λDU × TI) / 2 Where: λDU = Dangerous Undetected Failure Rate (failures/hour) TI = Proof Test Interval (hours)

1oo2 Architecture (1 out of 2):

PFDavg = (λDU × TI)² / 3 (Significantly lower than 1oo1 due to redundancy)

2oo3 Architecture (2 out of 3):

PFDavg = 3 × (λDU × TI)² / 2 - 2 × (λDU × TI)³ (Balanced redundancy with voting logic)
Common Failure Impact:

Common cause failures (β factor) can significantly reduce the effectiveness of redundancy. Typical β values: 2-10%. Must account for this in SIL verification calculations.

SIL Calculation Example

Scenario: High-High Level Trip (1oo2 transmitters)

Given:
• Transmitter λDU = 500 × 10⁻⁹ failures/hour (per SIL device database)
• Proof Test Interval (TI) = 8,760 hours (1 year)
• Architecture: 1oo2 (redundant)
• β factor = 5% (common cause)

Step 1: Calculate PFDavg without common cause PFDavg = (500×10⁻⁹ × 8760)² / 3 PFDavg = (0.00438)² / 3 = 6.4 × 10⁻⁵ Step 2: Add common cause contribution PFDavg(total) = PFDavg × (1 + β) PFDavg(total) = 6.4×10⁻⁵ × 1.05 = 6.7 × 10⁻⁵ Step 3: Determine SIL 6.7 × 10⁻⁵ falls within SIL 3 range (10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³) ✓ Transmitter subsystem achieves SIL 3

Emergency Shutdown (ESD) System

An ESD system is a specialized SIS designed to shut down equipment or processes in a controlled manner to prevent or mitigate emergency situations. Critical in oil & gas, petrochemical, and power industries.

ESD vs. BPCS:

ESD systems operate independently from process control. When activated, ESD takes priority and overrides normal control functions to achieve safe shutdown.

ESD Control Philosophy Framework

The ESD Control Philosophy is a comprehensive document defining:

  • Shutdown Hierarchy: ESD-1, ESD-2, ESD-3 levels with escalating scope
  • Initiating Causes: What conditions trigger each ESD level
  • Shutdown Sequences: Order of equipment isolation and de-energization
  • Response Times: Maximum time from detection to safe state
  • Depressurization Strategy: Blow-down rates and pressure relief
  • Restart Criteria: Conditions required before restart permitted

ESD Levels & Architecture

ESD-1: Process Unit Shutdown ▼

Scope: Individual process unit or equipment
Typical Causes:
• High/Low pressure in vessel
• High/Low level in separator
• High temperature in reactor
• Loss of cooling water
• Equipment vibration high

Actions:
• Close feed valves
• Stop pumps/compressors
• Open depressurization valves
• Activate fire suppression if required

Target SIL: Typically SIL 2

ESD-2: Plant Area Shutdown ▼

Scope: Multiple process units or entire plant section
Typical Causes:
• Major gas/liquid leak detected
• Fire in plant area
• Power failure
• Instrument air loss
• Multiple ESD-1 activations

Actions:
• All ESD-1 actions for affected units
• Isolate area from other plant sections
• De-energize electrical systems
• Close main inlet/outlet block valves
• Activate area deluge systems

Target SIL: Typically SIL 2-3

ESD-3: Total Plant Shutdown ▼

Scope: Entire facility shutdown
Typical Causes:
• Major external threat (seismic, tsunami)
• Total power blackout
• Complete utilities failure
• Security threat
• Management decision

Actions:
• All ESD-1 and ESD-2 actions plant-wide
• Isolate plant from external connections
• Activate emergency power for critical systems
• Full depressurization and flaring
• Muster personnel at assembly points

Target SIL: Typically SIL 3

ESD Logic Architecture

Voting Logic: Critical for balancing safety and spurious trips

ESD Valve Selection Criteria

  • Fail Position: Fail-safe position on loss of power/air (typically Fail Close or Fail Open)
  • Closure Time: Must meet required response time (typically 3-60 seconds)
  • Tight Shut-Off: Leakage class per API/ANSI standards
  • Partial Stroke Testing: Capability for online diagnostic testing
  • SIL Certification: Valve + actuator certified as assembly
  • Fire Safe: API 607 / API 6FA for hydrocarbon service
Critical Design Consideration:

ESD valve fail position must be determined through consequence analysis. Wrong fail position can create new hazards (e.g., fail-close on cooling water could cause overheating).

Fire & Gas (F&G) Detection System

Fire & Gas systems are specialized SIS designed to detect fire and hazardous gas releases early and initiate appropriate mitigation actions. Critical component of process safety in hydrocarbon facilities.

Integration Point:

F&G systems are typically integrated with ESD and HVAC systems for coordinated emergency response. Detection → Alarm → Mitigation → Shutdown sequence.

Fire & Gas Control Philosophy Components

  • Detection Strategy: Detector types, spacing, coverage zones
  • Voting Philosophy: Logic for alarm and action (1oo2, 2oo3, 2oo4)
  • Alarm Levels: Low, High, High-High with corresponding actions
  • Mitigation Actions: Deluge activation, HVAC shutdown, area isolation
  • Interface with ESD: When F&G triggers ESD levels
  • Testing & Maintenance: Functional test intervals, bump tests

Typical Implementation:

Gas Detection Voting ▼

2oo4 Voting (Typical):
• 4 detectors per zone
• 1 detector alarm → Operator notification only
• 2 detectors alarm → Audible/visual alarm + mitigating action
• 3+ detectors alarm → ESD activation

Alarm Levels:
• Low Alarm: 10-20% LEL → Investigate
• High Alarm: 20-40% LEL → Evacuate area
• High-High Alarm: 40-60% LEL → ESD + Mitigation

Rationale: Balance between safety (avoid missed detection) and availability (reduce spurious shutdowns)

Fire Detection Voting ▼

1oo2 or 2oo3 Voting (Typical):
Fire is immediate threat → faster response required

1oo2 Configuration:
• Any 1 detector confirms fire → Immediate alarm
• Confirmed by second method → Auto deluge activation

2oo3 Configuration:
• 2 out of 3 detectors → Confirmed fire
• Activates: Area alarm, deluge, ESD (if required), HVAC shutdown

Manual Override: Manual pull stations always 1oo1 (immediate action)

Mitigation Systems Integration

  • Deluge Systems: Water spray activation zones based on F&G detection
  • HVAC Control: Shutdown ventilation to prevent gas/smoke spread
  • Area Isolation: Close fire dampers, isolation valves
  • Depressurization: Reduce inventory in affected areas
  • ESD Activation: Trigger appropriate ESD level based on severity
  • Emergency Lighting: Activate escape route lighting

Risk Assessment Foundation

Risk assessment is the foundation for determining SIL requirements. It identifies hazards, evaluates consequences, estimates likelihood, and determines tolerable risk levels.

Risk = Likelihood × Consequence Acceptable Risk = Initiating Event Frequency ÷ Required RRF Required SIL determined from RRF target
Risk Assessment Hierarchy:

1) Identify hazards → 2) Analyze scenarios → 3) Evaluate consequences → 4) Estimate likelihood → 5) Determine risk level → 6) Specify SIL requirement

Systematic team-based examination using guide words to identify process deviations and their consequences.

LOPA (Layer of Protection Analysis)

Semi-quantitative method to determine if existing protection layers are sufficient or if additional SIS/SIF is required.

LOPA Formula:
Mitigated Event Frequency = IEF × PFD₁ × PFD₂ × ... × PFDₙ Where: IEF = Initiating Event Frequency (events/year) PFDᵢ = Probability of Failure on Demand for layer i If MEF > Tolerable Frequency → Additional IPL needed Required RRF = MEF / Tolerable Frequency

LOPA Worked Example

Scenario: Reactor overpressure due to cooling water failure
Step 1: Initiating Event Frequency (IEF) Cooling water pump failure = 0.1 per year Step 2: Identify Existing IPLs • BPCS high pressure alarm: PFD = 0.1 (operator response) • Pressure relief valve: PFD = 0.01 (mechanical) Step 3: Calculate Mitigated Event Frequency MEF = 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.01 = 0.0001 events/year = 1 × 10⁻⁴ events/year Step 4: Compare to Tolerable Risk Consequence: Catastrophic (potential fatality) Tolerable Frequency: 1 × 10⁻⁵ events/year (company criteria) Step 5: Determine if SIF Required MEF (1×10⁻⁴) > Tolerable (1×10⁻⁵) → YES, SIF needed Step 6: Calculate Required RRF Required RRF = MEF / Tolerable Required RRF = 1×10⁻⁴ / 1×10⁻⁵ = 10 Step 7: Determine SIL RRF = 10 → SIL 1 (RRF range 10-100) ✓ Implement SIL 1 high pressure trip SIF

Advanced PFD Calculations

Beyond basic formulas - accounting for real-world complexity:

Common Cause Failure (Beta Factor Method)

Redundancy is less effective than it appears due to common cause failures (CCF).

What is CCF?

Single event causing multiple channels to fail simultaneously: environmental (temperature, vibration), design error, maintenance error, external impact.

PFD Calculation Including Beta Factor: For 1oo2 (1 out of 2) Architecture: ──────────────────────────────────── PFD(independent) = (λDU × TI)² / 3 PFD(with CCF) = PFD(independent) × (1 - β) + β × (λDU × TI) Where β = fraction of failures that are common cause Typical β values: • Different manufacturers, locations: β = 2% • Same manufacturer, different models: β = 5% • Identical units, similar environment: β = 10% • Poor diversity, identical maintenance: β = 20%

Beta Factor Example Calculation

Given: 1oo2 Pressure Transmitters λDU = 500 × 10⁻⁹ failures/hour (each) TI = 8,760 hours (1 year) β = 10% (identical units, same location) Step 1: Independent failure PFD PFD(ind) = (500×10⁻⁹ × 8760)² / 3 PFD(ind) = (0.00438)² / 3 PFD(ind) = 6.4 × 10⁻⁶ Step 2: Common cause contribution PFD(CCF) = β × (λDU × TI) PFD(CCF) = 0.10 × 0.00438 PFD(CCF) = 4.38 × 10⁻⁴ Step 3: Total PFD PFD(total) = PFD(ind) × (1 - β) + PFD(CCF) PFD(total) = 6.4×10⁻⁶ × 0.9 + 4.38×10⁻⁴ PFD(total) = 5.76×10⁻⁶ + 4.38×10⁻⁴ PFD(total) = 4.44 × 10⁻⁴ Result: SIL 3 (10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³) WITHOUT CCF would be SIL 4 - HUGE difference!

Diagnostic Coverage Impact

Self-diagnostics reduce dangerous undetected failures:

λDU(effective) = λDU × (1 - DC) Where: λDU = Dangerous Undetected failure rate DC = Diagnostic Coverage (0 to 1) Diagnostic Coverage Levels: • DC < 60%: Low diagnostic coverage • 60% ≤ DC < 90%: Medium coverage • 90% ≤ DC < 99%: High coverage • DC ≥ 99%: Very high coverage Impact on SFF: SFF = (λS + λDD) / λTotal Higher DC → More λDD → Higher SFF → Lower SIL with same HFT
Example: Smart transmitter with 95% DC
Original: λDU = 200 FIT With DC: λDU(eff) = 200 × (1 - 0.95) = 10 FIT λDD = 200 × 0.95 = 190 FIT Result: 95% of dangerous failures now DETECTED This dramatically improves SFF and achievable SIL

Proof Test Coverage Factor

Not all proof tests are 100% effective:

PFD(average) = [λDU × TI × (1 - PTC)] / 2 + [λDU × MTTR × PTC] Where: PTC = Proof Test Coverage (0 to 1) MTTR = Mean Time To Repair after test finds failure Typical PTC values: • Full functional test, all failure modes: PTC = 99% • Partial stroke test (valves): PTC = 70-85% • Calibration check only: PTC = 50-60% • Online diagnostics: PTC = 30-50% Low PTC → Need more frequent testing or redundancy

Markov Modeling for Complex Systems

State-based probabilistic analysis for systems where failures interact:

System States for 1oo2 Configuration: ──────────────────────────────────────── State 0: Both channels OK (operational) State 1: One channel failed (operational, degraded) State 2: Both channels failed (dangerous failure) Transition Rates: State 0 → State 1: 2λ (either channel fails) State 1 → State 2: λ (remaining channel fails) State 1 → State 0: μ (repair brings back to State 0) PFD calculated from steady-state probability of State 2 Markov superior to simplified formulas when: • Repair times significant • Different failure rates per channel • Complex voting logic (2oo3, 2oo4) • Multiple degraded states exist

Mission Time Calculations

For systems where operation duration varies:

PFD(t) = 1 - e^(-λDU × t) For small λDU × t (typical): PFD(t) ≈ λDU × t Average over mission time T: PFD(avg) = (1/T) × ∫[0 to T] PFD(t) dt ≈ λDU × T / 2 (for constant λDU) For batch processes: • Mission time = Batch duration • Reset system between batches • Different calculation than continuous

Spurious Trip Rate (STR) Calculation

Economic impact of unwanted shutdowns:

STR = λS × (Number of Channels) For voting architectures: • 1oo1: STR = λS • 1oo2: STR = 2λS × (1 - PFD) (both must fail) • 2oo3: STR = 3λS² × TI (two must fail spuriously) Typical values: λS (safe failure rate) = 2-5 × λDU Annual spurious trips = STR × 8760 hours Cost = Trips/year × Cost per trip × Plant lifetime Balance safety (PFD) vs availability (STR) 2oo3 often optimal balance
Real Cost Example:

Refinery FCC unit shutdown: $500k-1M per event. If STR = 0.1 trips/year, cost = $100k/year. Over 20 years = $2M. Proper architecture selection saves millions.

Systematic Capability (Route 1H vs 2H)

Systematic failures (design, implementation errors) not covered by λDU:

Route 1H (Hardware Safety Integrity): • Achieve SIL through HFT and SFF alone • Maximum SIL 3 (IEC 61511) • Used for simple systems, proven in use Route 2H (Hardware + Software Safety Integrity): • Systematic capability from development process • Can achieve SIL 4 • Requires certified development per IEC 61508-3 • Software SIL = Hardware SIL (no shortcuts) Proven-in-Use (PIU): • Can reduce systematic capability requirements • Need: >10 years field data, >10⁹ operating hours • Configuration must match exactly • Rarely applicable for custom logic

Major Industrial Incidents - SIS Failures

Learning from catastrophic failures where SIS deficiencies played a critical role:

Why Study Failures?

Each incident represents lives lost, environmental damage, and regulatory changes. Understanding root causes prevents repetition. "Those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it."

Case Study 1: Buncefield (2005)

Location: Hertfordshire, UK | Date: December 11, 2005
Consequence: Massive vapor cloud explosion, 43 injuries, £1 billion damage
What Happened ▼

Incident Sequence:
• Gasoline storage tank (Tank 912) being filled overnight
• High level switch FAILED to activate (stuck in low position)
• Independent high-high level alarm ALSO FAILED (servo gauge mechanical fault)
• Tank overflowed for ~40 minutes, releasing ~300 tonnes gasoline
• Vapor cloud formed, found ignition source
• Explosion equivalent to 20-40 tonnes TNT
• 20 large storage tanks destroyed

SIS Failure Analysis ▼
ROOT CAUSES (SIS Related): ────────────────────────────────────── 1. High Level Switch Failure • Paddle switch stuck due to sediment/rust • NO proof testing performed • Last maintenance: NEVER since 1970s installation • λDU effectively 100% (failed state) 2. High-High Level Switch Failure • Servo gauge mechanical binding • Independent in theory, COMMON MODE in practice: - Same maintenance neglect - Same environmental conditions (corrosion) • β factor approached 100% (both failed) 3. NO SIL Assessment Ever Performed • System predated IEC 61511 • No SIL requirement determined • No PFD calculations • No systematic proof test program 4. Inadequate Independent Protection Layers • Needed: Flow totalizer with high limit • Needed: Overflow containment with detection • Needed: Tank level indication in control room • NONE were implemented
Lessons Learned ▼

✓ MANDATORY proof testing for ALL safety-critical level switches
✓ Independent diversity - different technology, different failure modes
✓ SIL assessment required for tank overfill protection (now API RP 2350)
✓ Operator visibility - tank level must be monitored in control room
✓ Flow totalizer as additional IPL for batching operations
✓ Maintenance is not optional for safety systems
✓ Common cause must be actively prevented, not assumed away

Case Study 2: Texas City Refinery (2005)

Location: Texas City, TX, USA | Date: March 23, 2005
Consequence: 15 fatalities, 180 injuries, $1.5 billion costs
What Happened ▼

Incident Sequence:
• Isomerization unit startup after maintenance
• Raffinate splitter tower OVERFILLED (3× normal level)
• Level transmitters indicating FALSELY (only measuring 0-9 feet range, level was >90 feet)
• Pressure relief routed to atmospheric blowdown stack (not flare)
• 55,000 gallons flammable liquid released to stack
• Vapor cloud ignited near temporary trailers
• 15 contractors killed in trailer explosion

SIS Failure Analysis ▼
ROOT CAUSES (SIS Related): ────────────────────────────────────── 1. Level Indication Inadequate • Level transmitter range: 0-9 feet • Tower height: 170 feet • BLIND to actual high level condition • SYSTEMIC DESIGN ERROR - wrong ranging 2. NO High Level SIF • No high-high level trip • No automatic feed shutoff • Relied SOLELY on operator monitoring • Operators DID NOT KNOW level was off-scale 3. Blowdown to Atmosphere (Not Flare) • Relief discharge to open stack • 113 feet tall - inadequate for dispersion • Near occupied trailers (personnel exposure) • Should have been routed to flare system 4. Alarms Ineffective • 11 alarms in 11 minutes • Alarm flood - operator overwhelmed • No alarm rationalization per ISA-18.2 • Critical alarms buried in nuisance alarms 5. NO SIL Rating of Level Protection • Never assessed as safety function • Treated as BPCS alarm only • Required SIL 2 or SIL 3 given consequence
Lessons Learned ▼

✓ Level indication must cover FULL operating range plus safety margin
✓ High-high level SIF mandatory for towers with overfill hazard
✓ Relief discharge to closed flare system, not atmosphere in populated areas
✓ Alarm management - rationalize, prioritize, reduce floods
✓ Occupied buildings must be sited away from high-consequence release zones
✓ Startup procedures require specific SIF verification steps
✓ Safety culture - production pressure cannot override safety
✓ Operator training on abnormal situations and SIF functionality

Case Study 3: Piper Alpha (1988)

Location: North Sea, UK | Date: July 6, 1988
Consequence: 167 fatalities, platform destroyed, worst offshore disaster
What Happened ▼

Incident Sequence:
• Condensate pump A removed for maintenance, blind flange left off
• Pump B tripped during evening shift
• Operators unaware of Pump A status (permit not communicated)
• Started Pump A with blind flange missing
• Massive gas release → explosion → oil fires
• Firewall penetrations not sealed properly - fire spread
• Gas detection system failed to prevent escalation
• Deluge system NOT activated (manual, required personnel on deck)
• Platform destroyed in 22 minutes

SIS Failure Analysis ▼
ROOT CAUSES (SIS Related): ────────────────────────────────────── 1. Gas Detection Inadequate • Detectors present but did NOT trigger shutdown • Set points too high to prevent nuisance trips • No automatic ESD linkage • Required manual operator action - too slow 2. ESD System Did Not Auto-Activate • Manual deluge activation only • No automatic fire/gas → deluge logic • Personnel could not reach activation points • Should have been 1oo2 auto-activation 3. Firewall Penetrations Not Sealed • Cable and pipe penetrations compromised walls • Fire spread between modules • Passive fire protection FAILED • Active fire suppression (deluge) FAILED 4. No Process Isolation Philosophy • Connected pipelines from other platforms continued pumping oil/gas • Fed the fire for hours • Needed automatic isolation on loss of comms • Other platforms should have ESD on Piper Alpha signal 5. Permit to Work System Failed • Systemic failure - not SIS but enabling cause • Led to initial gas release
Lessons Learned ▼

✓ F&G detection must auto-trigger ESD and deluge (no manual dependency)
✓ Firewall integrity - seal ALL penetrations properly
✓ Platform isolation - connected facilities must auto-shutdown on incident
✓ Temporary Refuge (TR) with independent life support required
✓ Permit to Work with robust communication of equipment status
✓ Safety Case regime introduced (UK) - demonstrate tolerable risk
✓ Voting logic for F&G - balance safety vs availability
✓ Deluge auto-activation - cannot rely on personnel in emergency

Control Philosophy Development

A Control Philosophy is a comprehensive document defining the operational strategy, safety logic, and functional requirements for process control and safety systems.

Why Philosophy Documents Matter:

The philosophy bridges engineering design and operations. It's the "constitution" for how the plant operates safely. Poor philosophy = unclear requirements = design errors = incidents.

  • Section 2: Process Description & P&IDs
  • Section 3: Operating Modes & States

How to Write an ESD Philosophy

Step 1: Define ESD Levels & Hierarchy ▼
ESD LEVEL DEFINITION TEMPLATE: ═══════════════════════════════════════ ESD-1: UNIT SHUTDOWN ──────────────────── Scope: Individual process unit [specify unit name/number] Objective: Safely shutdown unit while maintaining plant operation Initiation: • Automatic from process conditions • Manual from field push button • Manual from control room Actions Sequence: 1. Close unit feed valves [V-101, V-102] (0 sec) 2. Stop feed pumps [P-101A/B] (2 sec) 3. Open depressure valves [PV-201, PV-202] (5 sec) 4. Activate nitrogen purge [XV-301] (10 sec) 5. Stop agitators [M-401] (15 sec) 6. Isolate utilities [steam, cooling] (20 sec) Target Response Time: < 30 seconds to safe state Required SIL: SIL 2 (based on LOPA) Reset Conditions: • All process parameters normal • Manual reset by operator • Engineering authorization for critical shutdowns
Step 2: Identify All Initiating Causes ▼
INITIATING CAUSES CHECKLIST: ════════════════════════════ Process Parameters: □ High pressure (specify trip point) □ Low pressure (specify trip point) □ High temperature (specify trip point) □ Low temperature (specify trip point) □ High level (specify trip point) □ Low level (specify trip point) □ High flow (specify trip point) □ Low flow (specify trip point) Equipment Status: □ Pump failure (vibration, seal, bearing) □ Compressor trip (surge, vibration) □ Cooling water loss □ Instrument air loss □ Power failure □ Agitator failure Safety Systems: □ Fire detection (UV/IR flame detector) □ Gas detection (>20% LEL, confirmed) □ Toxic gas detection (H₂S > 10 ppm) □ Emergency stop push buttons □ Higher level ESD activation For EACH cause, specify: • Set point with engineering units • Time delay (if any) • Voting logic (1oo1, 1oo2, 2oo3) • SIL requirement
Step 3: Define Shutdown Sequence Logic ▼
SEQUENCE DEVELOPMENT METHOD: ════════════════════════════ 1. Identify Safe State What conditions define "safe"? • Equipment de-energized • Inventory reduced/isolated • Pressure relieved • Ignition sources removed • Atmosphere inerted 2. Work Backwards from Safe State What must happen last? (Purge complete) What before that? (Depressure) What before that? (Feed isolation) What first? (Stop feed addition) 3. Assign Timing • Consider process dynamics • Avoid waterhammer (gradual valve closure) • Coordinate steam/cooling sequencing • Allow time for reactions to complete safely 4. Define Interlocks • Permissive logic (e.g., can't start if...) • Sequential logic (must happen before...) • Override conditions (if any) Example Reactor Shutdown: ──────────────────────── T+0s: Close feed valves (5 sec ramp to avoid waterhammer) T+2s: Stop feed pumps (after valves closing) T+5s: Open reactor vent (controlled depressure) T+10s: Start cooling circulation (remove reaction heat) T+15s: Stop agitator (after heat removal started) T+20s: Activate nitrogen purge (inert atmosphere) T+30s: Safe state achieved Do NOT: ✗ Depressure before isolating feed (more inventory release) ✗ Stop cooling before reaction complete (runaway) ✗ Stop agitation while reacting (hot spots)

Step 5: Voting Logic Selection Criteria ▼
VOTING LOGIC DECISION TREE: ═══════════════════════════ Question 1: What is consequence of missed trip? ├─ Catastrophic (fatality possible) → Need redundancy │ └─ Go to Question 2 └─ Serious but not catastrophic → 1oo1 acceptable if SIL 1 Question 2: What is cost of spurious trip? ├─ Very high (>$500k/trip, safety impact) → Use 2oo3 or 2oo4 │ └─ Balance availability and safety └─ Moderate → Use 1oo2 └─ Good safety, acceptable availability Question 3: What SIL is required? ├─ SIL 3 → Must use 2oo3 minimum (with certified components) ├─ SIL 2 → 1oo2 usually sufficient (or 2oo3 for availability) └─ SIL 1 → 1oo1 acceptable (or 1oo2 for availability) Question 4: Is test bypass needed? ├─ Yes, for online testing → Use 2oo3 or higher │ └─ Can bypass one channel for test while maintaining safety └─ No, batch process → 1oo2 acceptable └─ Test during downtime RECOMMENDATION MATRIX: ───────────────────── High Consequence + High Spurious Cost → 2oo3 High Consequence + Low Spurious Cost → 1oo2 Low Consequence + High Spurious Cost → 2oo3 (availability driven) Low Consequence + Low Spurious Cost → 1oo1 Remember: More is not always better 2oo4 costs 4× components but only marginal safety improvement over 2oo3 Complexity increases failure modes Maintenance burden increases

PARTIAL BYPASS (One channel in redundant system) • Allowed for proof testing • Remaining channels must meet SIL • Maximum bypass time: [X] hours • Annunciate in control room 4. FORCED TRIP (Override permissives) • Emergency override - always allow trip • Cannot be defeated • Manual push buttons have priority Bypass Matrix Example: ───────────────────── SIF: High Pressure Trip (2oo3 transmitters) Allowed Bypasses: ✓ Bypass 1 transmitter for calibration (max 4 hours) → System remains 2oo2, still SIL 2 ✓ Inhibit trip during startup (max 30 minutes) → Requires supervisor approval → Flashing alarm in control room Prohibited Bypasses: ✗ Cannot bypass 2+ transmitters simultaneously ✗ Cannot inhibit during operation (only startup) ✗ Cannot disable alarms associated with SIF ✗ Cannot extend bypass beyond time limit without re-approval IEC 61511 Requirements: • All bypasses must be documented in Safety Manual • Bypass procedure must be written and approved • Operator training required • Bypass status visible to management • Bypass log reviewed monthly

SIL Verification Process

SIL verification demonstrates that the designed SIF meets the required SIL target determined from risk assessment.

IEC 61511 Requirement:

"The safety integrity of each SIF shall be verified to ensure that the specified safety integrity has been achieved."

Verification Methods Comparison

Method Applicability Accuracy Effort
Simplified Equations Low demand mode, standard architectures ± 30% Low
Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) Complex logic, multiple inputs ± 10% Medium
Reliability Block Diagram Series/parallel systems ± 10% Medium
Markov Models Repairable systems, degraded states ± 5% High
Monte Carlo Simulation Uncertainty quantification Confidence bounds High

Element 10: Management of Change (MOC) ▼

SIS Changes Requiring MOC:

□ Always require MOC:
   • New SIF addition or removal
   • SIL rating change
   • Trip setpoint modification
   • Voting logic change (1oo2 → 2oo3)
   • Component substitution (different model/manufacturer)
   • Proof test interval change

□ MOC Process for SIS:
   1. Justify change (technical basis)
   2. Re-verify SIL achievement
   3. Update PHA if risk changes
   4. Revise procedures and training
   5. Update documentation (SRS, P&IDs)
   6. Obtain multi-discipline approval
   7. Communicate to operations

Common Violation: "Like-for-like" replacement of SIS component without MOC when SIL certification differs

Element 11: Incident Investigation ▼

SIS Performance Tracking:

□ SIF Demand Logging
   • Record every SIF activation (genuine demand)
   • Analyze cause: Did upstream controls fail?
   • Calculate actual demand rate vs. design

□ Spurious Trip Analysis
   • Log all unwanted trips
   • Root cause investigation
   • Implement corrective actions

□ Failure to Operate Investigation
   • CRITICAL - treat as significant incident
   • Why didn't SIF trip when it should have?
   • Re-evaluate SIL adequacy
   • Update PFD calculations with real data

KPI to Track:
• SIF Availability = (Demands - Failures) / Demands
• Target: > 99% for SIL 2, > 99.9% for SIL 3

Error Prevention & Quality Assurance

Systematic approach to avoid the costly mistakes that lead to severe consequences:

Why Error Prevention Matters:

A single calculation error can result in inadequate SIL, leading to loss of life. Every engineer performing SIS work has a professional and ethical responsibility to get it right. This section provides the systematic checks used by expert practitioners.

Top 20 SIL Calculation Errors

Error Impact Check Method
1. Using wrong units for λDU Off by 10³-10⁹ Always verify: failures/hour vs FIT
2. Forgetting to include beta factor Overestimate safety 2-10× CCF mandatory for all redundant systems
3. Using equipment λ instead of λDU Includes safe failures, wrong PFD Verify λDU specifically (dangerous undetected)
4. Wrong formula for architecture Completely wrong PFD Cross-reference ISA TR84.00.02 Table 4
5. Mixing low/high demand mode formulas Wrong metric (PFD vs PFH) Check demand rate: >1/yr = high demand
6. Not accounting for diagnostics Conservative estimate, wastes money Apply DC factor to reduce effective λDU
7. Wrong proof test interval PFD off by TI ratio Verify: hours, not years. 1 yr = 8,760 hr
8. Arithmetic errors in calculation Wrong final PFD Use calculator twice, independent check
9. Ignoring HFT constraints Claim unachievable SIL Check SFF vs HFT table (IEC 61508 Table 2)
10. Using wrong λDU source Unreliable failure rate data Use only: SIL certificates, SERH, Exida FMEDA
11. Not adding all subsystems Forget sensor, logic, or valve PFD Verify: PFDtot = PFDsen + PFDlog + PFDfinal
12. Wrong SIL range interpretation Claim wrong SIL SIL 2 is 10⁻³ to <10⁻², not 10⁻² to 10⁻³
13. Forgetting MTTR contribution Underestimate PFD For repairable: add λDU × MTTR term
14. Using nominal not max λDU Optimistic PFD Use upper confidence bound (e.g., 70th %ile)
15. Not accounting for proof test coverage Actual PFD higher than calculated Apply PTC factor or use partial test interval
16. Assuming independence without verification Hidden common cause Check: power, I/O, software, environment
17. Neglecting systematic failures PFD calculation not enough Verify systematic capability (Route 1H/2H)
18. Wrong interpretation of SFF Incorrect architectural limit SFF = (λS + λDD) / λtotal, not just λS/λtotal
19. Using PFD for continuous mode Wrong reliability metric Continuous mode requires PFH, not PFD
20. Transcription errors (copy/paste) Use wrong value from database Double-check every number entered

Pre-Submission Verification Checklist

Use this before finalizing ANY SIL calculation:

□ Architecture correctly identified (1oo1, 1oo2, 2oo3, etc.)
□ Formula matches architecture (cross-ref ISA TR84.00.02)
□ All λDU values in same units (failures/hour preferred)
□ λDU sources documented (SIL cert, database, FMEDA)
□ Beta factor included for redundant systems
□ Beta value justified (2-20% typical, document why)
□ Proof test interval correct (hours, verified against plan)
□ Diagnostic coverage applied if applicable
□ All three subsystems calculated (sensor + logic + final)
□ Arithmetic double-checked (use calculator twice)
□ SIL range verified (PFD within correct decade)
□ HFT constraint checked (SFF vs HFT table)
□ Systematic capability addressed (Route 1H or 2H)
□ Units clearly stated in final result
□ Assumptions documented (mission time, etc.)
□ Calculation signed and dated by engineer
□ Peer review performed (independent check)
□ Result makes sense (sanity check vs expectations)

Peer Review Process

Independent verification is mandatory for SIL 2 and above:

PEER REVIEW PROCEDURE: ═══════════════════════ Step 1: Select Qualified Reviewer ─────────────────────────────────── • Different person than original engineer • Competent in SIL calculations • No conflict of interest Step 2: Provide Complete Package ───────────────────────────────── □ SIF description and requirements □ Architecture diagram □ Component data sheets □ Calculation worksheet □ Assumptions documented Step 3: Reviewer Checks ──────────────────────── □ Verify formula selection □ Recalculate independently (don't just check arithmetic) □ Validate component data sources □ Check beta factor reasonableness □ Verify SIL conclusion □ Check against common errors list Step 4: Document Review ──────────────────────── • Review comments documented • Discrepancies resolved • Reviewer signs off • Original engineer addresses all comments Step 5: Final Approval ────────────────────── • Both engineer and reviewer sign • Calculation marked "VERIFIED" • Filed in project documentation REJECTION CRITERIA: ────────────────── If reviewer finds errors affecting SIL level conclusion, calculation MUST be revised and re-reviewed.

Sanity Check Methods

Quick verification that results are reasonable:

Check Method
Order of magnitude SIL 1: ~10⁻², SIL 2: ~10⁻³, SIL 3: ~10⁻⁴
If you calculated 10⁻¹ for SIL 2, something's wrong
Redundancy benefit 1oo2 should be ~10-100× better than 1oo1
If barely better, check beta factor
Dominant term Usually valves > sensors > logic
If logic dominates, verify certified PFD
Test interval impact Doubling TI should ~double PFD for 1oo1

If no impact, not applied correctly
Component comparison Similar components should have similar λDU

IEC 61508 - Functional Safety Foundation

  • Scope: Generic standard for electrical/electronic/programmable safety systems
  • Parts: 7-part standard covering entire lifecycle
  • SIL Definition: Establishes SIL 1-4 classification
  • Application: Basis for all sector-specific standards
  • Latest Edition: IEC 61508:2010 (Edition 2.0)

IEC 61511 - Process Industry SIS

  • Scope: Safety instrumented systems for process industry sector
  • Parts: 3-part standard (Framework, Guidelines, Guidance)
  • Key Topics: SIS lifecycle, SIL determination, verification, management
  • Process-Specific: Addresses demands, proof testing, bypasses
  • Latest Edition: IEC 61511:2016 (Edition 2.0)
  • 2026 Addendum: Cybersecurity integration guidance

ISA-84 / ANSI/ISA-84.00.01

North American adaptation of IEC 61511:

  • Harmonized with IEC 61511-1:2016
  • Additional clarifications for US industry practices
  • Widely adopted in North America
